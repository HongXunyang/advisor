Introduction

ADVISOR determines the crystal orientation matrix UB from two measured Bragg reflections. To validate it against the software used at the beamline, a beamline scientist at ESRF ID20 ran the same 36 two-reflection cases through BLISS (18 cubic, 16 tetragonal, 2 monoclinic crystals, 7.5–13.5 keV). Each row of bliss_ub_test.csv holds the inputs (energy, lattice, two reflections with θ, χ, φ, 2θ), ADVISOR's UB (UB_ij, computed with ADVISOR's original Kabsch method) and BLISS's UB (uij). This report establishes the convention mapping between the two programs, quantifies the residual discrepancy, explains it, and documents the tolerances used in test_bliss_ub_comparison.py.

How the UB matrix is computed

For each reflection \(i\) with Miller indices \(\mathbf h_i\), ADVISOR builds the lattice vector and the measured scattering vector in the sample (φ-axis) frame:

$$\mathbf g_i = B\,\mathbf h_i,\qquad \mathbf q_i = R(\theta_i,\chi_i,\phi_i)^{\mathsf T}\,\mathbf Q^{\text{lab}}_i,\qquad R = R_z(\theta)\,R_y(\chi)\,R_x(\phi),$$

where the columns of \(B\) are \(\mathbf a^*,\mathbf b^*,\mathbf c^*\) (with the factor \(2\pi\)), and \(\mathbf Q^{\text{lab}} = k\,(-\sin 2\theta,\ \cos 2\theta-1,\ 0)\) with \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\). Both methods then find a rotation \(U\) with \(\mathbf q_i \approx U\mathbf g_i\) and report \(UB = U\,B\).

Busing–Levy (ADVISOR default, and what BLISS uses). Reflection 1 is matched exactly, and reflection 2 fixes the rotation about it:

$$T(\mathbf a,\mathbf b) = \big[\,\hat{\mathbf a},\ \hat{\mathbf t}\times\hat{\mathbf a},\ \hat{\mathbf t}\,\big],\quad \hat{\mathbf t} = \frac{\mathbf a\times\mathbf b}{\lVert\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\rVert},\qquad U = T(\mathbf q_1,\mathbf q_2)\;T(\mathbf g_1,\mathbf g_2)^{\mathsf T}.$$

Kabsch (ADVISOR option). A least-squares fit over all reflections, solved by SVD:

$$U = \arg\min_{U\in SO(3)}\sum_i \lVert \mathbf q_i - U\mathbf g_i\rVert^2:\qquad \sum_i \mathbf g_i\mathbf q_i^{\mathsf T} = W\Sigma V^{\mathsf T},\quad U = V\,\mathrm{diag}\!\big(1,1,\det VW^{\mathsf T}\big)\,W^{\mathsf T}.$$

BLISS applies the same Busing–Levy construction in its own lab frame, so UBBLISS = P·UBADVISOR, with P given in section 1.

1 · Coordinate convention

For every row, the BLISS matrix is ADVISOR's matrix with its rows rearranged: u1j = UB_2j, u2j = −UB_1j, u3j = UB_3j. In matrix form

Because the rows of UB are the lab-frame (φ-axis-frame) components of Q, a row permutation with a sign is purely a relabelling of the laboratory axes; the columns (the reciprocal-lattice basis, i.e. the B matrix) are untouched. P is a proper rotation, so both frames are right-handed and share the vertical z axis. ADVISOR puts the beam along +Y; BLISS's +x is ADVISOR's +Y, i.e. the beam direction, and BLISS's +y is ADVISOR's −X. Both sketches below show the same physical setup seen from above.

The mapping was found without assuming it. For each row, all 48 signed 3×3 permutations M were tried, and the one minimising max|M·UBADVISOR − UBBLISS| was taken. P won on every row, and the runner-up was always at least 10× worse. The two monoclinic rows (β = 109.04°) agree too, so the B-matrix conventions are the same in both programs.

2 · Why the numbers still differ: Busing–Levy vs Kabsch

After applying P, ADVISOR's CSV values still differ from BLISS by up to 1.1e-02 Å⁻¹ (0.56° of misorientation). The cause is the orientation algorithm, not a bug:

  • BLISS – Busing–Levy (Acta Cryst. 22, 457, 1967): reflection 1 is the primary and is matched exactly; reflection 2 (secondary) only fixes the rotation about it.
  • ADVISOR (original) – Kabsch: the rotation that best fits both reflections in the least-squares sense.
— measured scattering vector q‒ ‒ lattice vector after rotation, U·g

If the measured angle between the two reflections equals the angle implied by the lattice constants, the two methods give the same U. Real data never match perfectly: lattice constants are entered to a few decimals, and motor positions carry their own errors. Kabsch spreads the mismatch Δ over both reflections, while Busing–Levy puts all of it on reflection 2, so the two U differ by at most |Δ|. To confirm this, a Busing–Levy mode was added to ADVISOR. It is now the default, and Kabsch stays selectable from the UB Matrix panel. With Busing–Levy, the largest difference from BLISS over all 36 rows is 8.7e-08 Å⁻¹.

How close is that? The BLISS values in the CSV were supplied with 5–9 decimals (mostly 8). Every Busing–Levy difference is smaller than one unit in the last digit supplied for that value (worst: 0.87 units, line 2 u31 = -0.4435863, given to 7 decimals). All but 1 entry is within half a unit, as expected from rounding; the exception would fit truncation or a dropped digit. That the remaining difference comes from the precision of the supplied numbers is an inference. It has not been checked against BLISS's own full-precision output.

What the columns mean
Line · Crystal · Reflections
The CSV line, the crystal system implied by the lattice constants, and the two reflections (H K L), in the order they were used. Reflection 1 is Busing–Levy's primary.
Angle mismatch
The lattice-predicted minus the measured angle between the two reflections. This is the only kind of input error that makes Busing–Levy and Kabsch disagree. It is 0 for perfectly consistent data.
Max |ΔUB| vs BLISS
The largest of the nine element differences between ADVISOR's UB (rotated into BLISS's frame with P) and BLISS's UB, in Å⁻¹. For Busing–Levy this is the validation result: in this run, every value is below the 10⁻⁶ Å⁻¹ tolerance. For Kabsch it shows the size of the method difference (tolerance 0.02 Å⁻¹).
Misorientation vs BLISS
The angle of the rotation that takes ADVISOR's orientation onto BLISS's: one number for how far apart the two orientations are, independent of the lattice. Busing–Levy values are at numerical-noise level. "< 10⁻⁶" means the angle is below what this calculation can resolve, which is about 10⁻⁶°. On every row, the Kabsch value is no larger than the angle mismatch (see the scatter plot in section 2).

6 · Conclusion

  • Convention. ADVISOR and BLISS use the same reciprocal-lattice (B) convention, and their lab frames differ by one fixed rotation: UBBLISS = P·UBADVISOR, a −90° turn about the vertical axis, because BLISS puts x along the beam while ADVISOR puts Y along it. No handedness or sign problem exists.
  • Agreement. With the new Busing–Levy mode (now ADVISOR's default), ADVISOR agrees with BLISS to ≤ 8.7e-08 Å⁻¹ on all 36 cases, smaller than one unit in the last digit supplied for every value. ADVISOR's original Kabsch fit stays within 0.56°, and each row's gap is bounded by how inconsistent that row's two reflections are with the lattice.
  • Regression protection. tests/bliss_validation/test_bliss_ub_comparison.py enforces all of the above (146 checks, all passing in this run). Re-run this generator after adding new BLISS rows to the CSV.
